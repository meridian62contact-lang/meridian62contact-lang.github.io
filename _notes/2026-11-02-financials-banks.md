### What happened

On 27 October, HSBC reported third-quarter (3Q26) profit before tax up 32% to US$9.6bn compared with the third quarter of last year, which included a $1.1bn charge for a lawsuit linked to Bernard Madoff [1][2]. The Financial Times reported that this narrowly missed forecasts, with revenue of $18.6bn also below expectations [2]. Disappointment among investors stems from higher expectations due to increasing interest rates [2]. London-listed shares fell as much as 7% before closing 5.4% lower at 1,291.4p [5], despite higher profit and revenue. Additionally, HSBC repeated rather than raised its 2026 <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-guidance">guidance</a>, including banking <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-net-interest-income-nii">net interest income (NII)</a> of at least $46bn [1].

### What people are missing

Behavioural science suggests why a 32% profit rise can still disappoint. <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-prospect-theory">Prospect theory</a>, developed by Kahneman and Tversky, posits that people judge outcomes as gains or losses against a reference point rather than in absolute terms [3]. The reference point here appears to be forecasts. The FT reports that investors had expected a bigger windfall from higher interest rates [2], and chief financial officer Pam Kaur said that in a high-interest-rate environment there is sometimes a market expectation that "we upgrade guidance or we change our targets" [2]. HSBC did not do so. The fall therefore reflects the gap between results and expectations: a 32% rise in profits may be read as a shortfall, and prospect theory maintains that losses weigh more heavily than equal gains [3].

### Why it matters

HSBC is one of Europe's largest banks [2]. The share-price fall coincided with a decline of more than 1% in the <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-stoxx-600-banks-index">Stoxx 600 Banks Index</a> on 27 October, pulling European lenders lower [2]. The results also show how the bank is earning from higher interest rates. It says its NII grew because of deposit growth and the reinvestment of its <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-structural-hedge">structural hedge</a> at higher yields, while its <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-net-interest-margin">net interest margin</a> was stable [1]. The wider reaction suggests investors are treating HSBC's results as a read on how large banks are turning higher rates into profit.

### What we are watching next

We are watching HSBC's full-year results for whether it meets its reiterated 2026 targets of, for example, banking NII of at least $46bn [1]. Results in line with these targets, with the shares still lagging, would support the presence of high market expectations rather than a change in the bank's outlook, while a reduction in targets would weaken this interpretation. The Budget announced on 28 October also raised the UK <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-bank-surcharge">bank surcharge</a> from 3% to 6% for 15 months from January 2027 [6], though most of HSBC's profit is earned in Asia, so the change reaches only part of the bank [2].
