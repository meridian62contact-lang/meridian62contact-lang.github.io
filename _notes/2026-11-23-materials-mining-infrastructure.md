### What happened

On 9 November, several cities in China's Hebei province, including the steel hubs of Tangshan and Handan, issued red or orange <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-heavy-pollution-alert">heavy-pollution alerts</a> and brought in emergency curbs. <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-sintering">Sintering</a> plants were among the operations told to cut back, and a survey of local mills pointed to sintering cuts of 20% to 50% [1]. Iron ore futures in Singapore fell to a four-year low in the week of 16 November and were trading near US$88 per tonne on 18 November [2]. China accounted for 47% of Vale's revenue last quarter [4], so the obvious reading is that less Chinese steel means less demand for Vale's ore.

### What people are missing

The curbs target one step in particular. In 2020, sintering was the biggest source of air pollution in Chinese steelmaking, producing 71% of the industry's sulphur dioxide, 73% of its nitrogen oxides and 54% of its fine particle emissions [5]. <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-iron-ore-fines">Iron ore fines</a> are too small to go straight into a <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-blast-furnace">blast furnace</a>, so mills first heat them until the particles fuse into larger pieces. <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-pellets">Pellets</a> are rolled into balls and fired before they leave the pellet plant, so they arrive ready for the furnace [6]. For pellets, the heating step has already happened in Brazil or Oman, not in Hebei. That is why earlier sintering curbs in Hebei pushed mills to use more pellets and <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-lump-ore">lump ore</a> [7].

Vale sells both, but mostly fines: they made up seven of every eight tonnes it sold in the third quarter. Its fines price fell 10% from a year earlier to US$85.1 per tonne, whilst its pellet price rose 2% to US$134.0 [4]. Each tonne of pellets earned US$59.2 of EBITDA, more than twice the roughly US$28 earned on a tonne of fines [4]. Pellet <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-premium">premiums</a> roughly doubled between mid-2025 and August 2026, mainly because supply from other producers was disrupted [8]. Even so, Vale's São Luís pellet plant produced nothing in the quarter. Vale says the plant runs in line with demand and that it will decide on a full restart based on market conditions [4].

### Why it matters

The curbs hit the product Vale sells most of, so the short-term damage is real. But they also show why steelmakers pay more for ore that skips the sinter plant. Vale already plans to roughly double its output of pellets and <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-briquettes">briquettes</a>, from 30–34 million tonnes in 2026 to 60–70 million tonnes by 2030 [9], and every winter smog season strengthens that case. The limit is scale. Pellets were only 11% of Vale's revenue last quarter [4], so they cannot make up for weaker fines prices on their own. And if Chinese steel output keeps falling, demand for every type of ore falls with it. Chinese research firm SMM expected November's curbs to give prices only a limited lift for this reason [8].

### What we are watching next

Vale holds its annual investor event, Vale Day, in December [10]. We are watching whether it keeps its 2030 target for pellets and briquettes, what pellet volumes it guides for 2027, and whether São Luís restarts. Keeping the target with iron ore at a four-year low would suggest Vale sees the move away from sinter feed as lasting, not just a winter effect.
