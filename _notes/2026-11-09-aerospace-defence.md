### What happened

Democrats won control of the Senate and were poised to win the House in the 3 November <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-midterms">midterms</a> [1]. Before the vote, the White House had asked for $1.5 trillion for defence in 2027, including $350 billion through a <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-party-line-bill-reconciliation">party-line bill</a>; the Senate's top Democratic appropriator called the total request a "nonstarter" [2]. Investors did not treat the result as a threat to RTX: the stock edged higher into the vote, trading at $203.70 on 3 November, up $0.91 on the day [3], a small move, but not the fall that a threat to defence budgets might suggest.

### What people are missing

The real risk for defence stocks sits in the $350 billion party-line <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-add-on">add-on</a>, not the $1.15 trillion <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-base-budget">base budget</a>, and a Democratic Congress makes that add-on hard to pass [2], because a party-line bill needs only one party's votes and Democrats now hold the Senate. RTX is less exposed than most peers: Trefis rates its exposure as medium, against medium-high for Lockheed Martin, and its commercial engine and <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-aftermarket">aftermarket</a> business does not depend on Congress [2]. Demand for its missile systems, including <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-patriot">Patriot</a>, Standard Missile and Tomahawk, also stays high while the Iran conflict is unresolved, with Trump saying he may have to give Iran "the hammer" as Qatari-mediated talks on reopening the Strait of Hormuz continued [4]. The mechanism is therefore about timing. Congress controls when money is released for orders, not whether the military needs the missiles, so a Democratic Congress can slow the add-on without removing the demand behind it.

### Why it matters

About 60% of <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-exit-poll">exit-poll</a> voters said attacking Iran was the wrong decision [1], so the new Congress is likely to question war funding, including the <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-unfunded-requirement">unfunded</a> munitions bill that would pay for Raytheon <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-interceptor">interceptor</a> orders. From January 2027, when the new Congress sits, Democrats gain greater leverage and <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-congressional-oversight">oversight</a>, with Trump acknowledging the impact of the Iran war [5]. For an ordinary shareholder, that most likely means RTX's orders arrive later than the administration planned, which can push back revenue, but it does not mean the orders disappear.

### What we are watching next

The first test is <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-full-year-appropriations">full-year appropriations</a> before the <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-stopgap-funding">stopgap</a> expires on 11 December 2026, which will show whether interceptor funding is delayed or simply pushed into the new year, while CFO remarks at the Baird conference on 10 November 2026 will show whether RTX itself sees a timing risk. The main challenge to our reading is Europe: if other buyers follow Denmark's choice of Europe's <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-samp-t">SAMP/T</a> over Patriot, demand is less secure than we think [6].
