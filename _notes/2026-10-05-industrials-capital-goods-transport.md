### What happened

Siemens reported record <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-orders">orders</a> of €27.9bn in Q3 FY2026, up 14% on a <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-comparable-basis">comparable basis</a> from €24.7bn a year earlier [1]. The increase was led by Smart Infrastructure, where data-centre orders recorded triple-digit growth over the first nine months of FY2026 [1]. At the same time, Siemens' total order <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-backlog">backlog</a> reached a record €132bn, alongside quarterly revenue of €20.8bn, up 8% on a comparable basis [1]. This growth coincides with a new partnership Siemens formed with Rittal in March 2026 to jointly develop power-distribution and cooling infrastructure for AI data centres [2].

### What people are missing

This jump in record orders alongside data-centre demand poses an interesting question: while it implies demand for Siemens' equipment is strong, it doesn't reveal whether Siemens can turn the order book into delivered, revenue-generating projects at the same speed — a gap underlined by orders growing nearly twice as fast as revenue (14% vs 8%).

AI chips require far more power than previous hardware generations. Because a chip does no mechanical work, the electrical power it draws is released as heat — power in equals heat out. AI workloads also require chips to "talk" to each other at extremely high speeds, which only happens when they sit physically close together, so chips are packed tightly onto a <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-rack">rack</a>. As more chips are packed in, the rack's total <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-power-draw">power draw</a>, and therefore the heat it releases, rises in proportion. Siemens itself states that AI data-centre <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-power-density">power densities</a> now regularly exceed 100 kW per rack — roughly ten times the 8–12 kW traditional racks were built for — and could exceed 1 MW by 2030, a jump existing cooling and power-distribution infrastructure was never designed to handle [2][3].

Air cannot carry this heat away fast enough once rack density passes a certain point, which is why data centres are now shifting to liquid cooling with Rittal's help [2]: a liquid-cooled <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-cold-plate">cold plate</a> pressed onto a chip removes heat roughly 300 times faster than air blown across it [3]. That switch cannot simply be scaled up overnight — an order can be signed in a day, but the liquid-cooling equipment still needs to be designed, tested, certified and manufactured before it can be installed, which takes far longer. That the partnership was needed at all, rather than Siemens and Rittal simply producing more of their existing air-cooling equipment, shows this is a genuine engineering challenge rather than a question of selling more units [2] — a strong signal that converting this slice of the backlog into revenue will take longer, or be less predictable, than Siemens' more established product lines.

### Why it matters

This uncertainty extends beyond Siemens' own delivery capability: the hyperscalers and data-centre operators placing these orders are themselves committing vast sums of <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-capital-expenditure">capex</a> to AI infrastructure without yet proving it will generate a sufficient return. Siemens acknowledged this directly on its Q3 FY2026 earnings call, flagging potential <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-volatility">volatility</a> in data-centre order intake due to the lumpiness of large contracts, which could affect revenue visibility going forward [4]. For Siemens, the key question is how quickly its growing data-centre backlog turns into sales, since delivery constraints could make revenue growth less predictable [1]. Beyond that, stronger orders only translate into stronger earnings if Siemens can deliver them profitably, rather than simply building a larger backlog.

### What we are watching next

At Siemens' next results, we will be watching how quickly its record backlog converts into revenue and whether Smart Infrastructure can hold its margins as data-centre orders are delivered, plus any further disclosure on the size and timing of those orders.
