### What happened

On 2 October, Northrop Grumman introduced its new chip "Fortitude", promising improvements in satellites, radar, GPS, and <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-electronic-warfare">electronic warfare</a> [1], making it more competitive for future contract bids. The company has also continued to secure multiple new contracts over the past two weeks worth over $300 million [2], yet its stock price is still down 14.94% <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-ytd-year-to-date">year to date</a>, and down over 4% since 28 September [3].

### What people are missing

The falling stock price comes after record highs hit in March as the US–Iran war began, trading at around $768.20 per share versus $585.66 per share just two months prior, so a dip below those highs is not abnormal — yet it is notable that NOC shares have continued to tumble well past their pre-war levels [3]. An important aspect of this is recognising that new contracts don't directly translate into new cash flow or profit: almost all contracts awarded will only see growth in three to four years, since the contracts run for long periods, often until 2030, with payment completed towards the later stages of the projects [2]. Furthermore, early production can be costly and lead to <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-cost-overrun">cost overruns</a>, and as most contracts are <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-firm-fixed-price-contract">firm-fixed-price</a>, meaning the company is responsible for any additional costs, profit could be reduced or turn into a loss [2]. Investors need to weigh these risks, which is why positive contract news does not always translate into a higher share price.

### Why it matters

The timing matters given the midterms and American elections, as well as the US halting attacks on Iran whilst investing heavily in defence. It also matters because Congress has not passed funding for the full year: as a result, the growth generated by these contracts, and the growth expected from the new chip, is not guaranteed, and the <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-stopgap-funding">stopgap</a> keeps spending flat until 11 December [4]. While these awards look positive, they are partly existing contracts moving forward rather than purely new business. For shareholders, this means Northrop Grumman's <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-order-book">order book</a> depends heavily on a congressional vote and on demand; Fortitude's commercial promise sits behind this timing. The role of defence contracts more broadly is highlighted by last week's attack: the Houthis struck Riyadh airport, killing 12 people and wounding over 300 [5]. This attack shows why demand for missile defence is strong — it could mean the US joins Saudi Arabia's fight against the Houthis. This is relevant for investors because Northrop will report its third-quarter results on 20 October [6].

### What we are watching next

A key factor to watch is the <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-midterms">midterm</a> election results on 3 November; depending on which party takes control of the House or the Senate, it will significantly affect how much is allocated to the new defence budget. This should give investors more clarity on how secure contract payments are, as well as a sense of whether many new contracts can be awarded.
