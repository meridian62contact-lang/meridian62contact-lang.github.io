### What happened

Cheniere Energy (NYSE: LNG) has signed a 22-year agreement to supply Petrobras with about 0.8 million tonnes per annum (<a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-mtpa">mtpa</a>) of <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-lng">liquefied natural gas (LNG)</a> on a <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-free-on-board-fob">free-on-board</a> basis [1]. Chief executive Jack Fusco said the contract gives fixed-fee cash-flow visibility "to underpin further <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-brownfield">brownfield</a> liquefaction capacity growth" [1], and it came days after staff at the US Federal Energy Regulatory Commission (<a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-ferc">FERC</a>) issued the final environmental impact statement for Cheniere's Sabine Pass Stage 5 expansion [2]. The obvious reading is that demand for US LNG is strong enough for customers to commit into the late 2040s.

### What people are missing

The evidence suggests Cheniere's binding limit is not customers but how much heat its plants can pull out of natural gas. Its liquefaction trains use the ConocoPhillips <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-optimised-cascade-process">Optimised Cascade process</a>, a series of cascading refrigeration circuits using single-component <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-refrigerant">refrigerants</a>. Each circuit is driven by large compressors, and every 1% change in compressor power shifts LNG output by roughly 0.8–1% [3][4]. On the hot US Gulf Coast, that power is squeezed from two sides. The gas turbines driving the compressors lose about 0.7% of their power for each 1°C rise in air temperature [5]. And because a liquid's <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-vapour-pressure">vapour pressure</a> climbs steeply with temperature (the <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-clausius-clapeyron-relation">Clausius–Clapeyron relation</a>), a warmer day forces the compressors to push refrigerant vapour to a higher pressure before it condenses, which takes more work and lowers summer output [6]. Upgrading existing trains can only add so much: Cheniere counts about 5 mtpa in operation [1], while Sabine Pass Stage 5 alone would add up to about 20 mtpa, built in two phases [7]. This suggests the Petrobras volumes are best read, as Cheniere itself frames them, as commercial support for capacity that still needs approval — not a call on spare output.

### Why it matters

Cheniere can only produce as much gas as its cooling equipment allows. That means growing its exports over the long term depends on building new plants, not on finding new customers. Long contracts like this one help pay for that construction, but the extra gas only arrives once the expansion is approved and built, with first operations expected by 2030 [8]. Until then, hot weather can trim what the existing plants produce [6], however much customers want.

### What we are watching next

FERC's final decision on Sabine Pass Stage 5, which Cheniere has said it expects in late 2026, ahead of a final investment decision in early 2027 [9]. Approval on that timetable would support our reading that new capacity, not demand, sets Cheniere's pace; a delay would leave new contracts waiting on gas that cannot yet be made.
