### What happened

On 12 November 2026, Cerebras Systems (NASDAQ: CBRS) reported third-quarter revenue of $210.8m, up 55% year on year, while its cloud and other services revenue grew 265% to $141.9m [1]. The AI chipmaker also reported $26.1bn in remaining performance obligations, representing revenue expected from existing contracts [2]. Much of this comes from its agreement with OpenAI to provide 750 megawatts (MW) of computing capacity for AI inference, the process of generating responses from trained AI models [3]. Following stronger results and raised guidance, Cerebras shares rose more than 17% in extended trading [4]. However, the same quarterly filing revealed that the company had already fallen behind parts of its agreed delivery schedule [2].

### What people are missing

The immediate constraint is Cerebras's ability to bring data centres online. On its earnings call, chief executive Andrew Feldman attributed the delays to land availability, power connections and building construction, stating that the company already had the necessary materials [5]. This reflects a wider industry problem: the International Energy Agency has warned that electricity infrastructure and equipment shortages are restricting data-centre development [6].

For Cerebras, however, these delays carry specific financial consequences. Its OpenAI agreement requires 250 MW of capacity by the end of 2026, another 250 MW in 2027 and the final 250 MW in 2028, with additional milestones along the way [7]. Its latest filing disclosed that, by 30 September, deliveries were already behind schedule before accounting for potentially excused delays [2].

The contract does not simply allow Cerebras to deliver late and extend the service period. Each delayed portion loses an equivalent number of days from its contracted service term. Unexcused delays remaining unresolved 30 days after specified milestones trigger service credits, reducing OpenAI's future payments, while a 90-day delay allows either party to cancel the affected portion [2]. Cerebras disclosed that credits began accruing from 1 October before adjustments for excused delays [2].

Importantly, management has already deducted estimated delay credits from the reported $26.1bn figure and confirmed that no capacity had been cancelled for delays as of its earnings call [5]. The figure therefore reflects management's current estimates rather than ignoring the problem entirely.

### Why it matters

The distinction between contracted revenue and profitable revenue is particularly important. Cerebras's remaining performance obligations include estimated pass-through costs, such as data-centre rent and electricity, which are charged to customers but may contribute little additional profit [2]. The $26.1bn figure therefore does not represent an equivalent amount of future earnings.

Customer concentration compounds the risk. OpenAI contributed 34% of third-quarter revenue and Mohamed bin Zayed University of Artificial Intelligence another 38%, leaving Cerebras heavily dependent on two customers [2]. Delays could reduce revenue from its largest contracts even if demand remains strong.

Nevertheless, the immediate cancellation risk appears limited: Cerebras estimates that less than 5% of its committed OpenAI capacity could become eligible for termination during 2026 [2]. Management also expects to catch up with its delivery schedule in the second half of 2027 [5]. The question is whether it can recover before further contractual costs accumulate.

### What we are watching next

We will watch whether Cerebras meets its year-end capacity commitments, whether estimated service credits increase and whether its recovery remains on track for the second half of 2027. Fourth-quarter results will offer another test: management forecasts core revenue of $310m–$330m and core gross margins of 47%–49%, both adjusted measures [1]. Successful deployment with limited additional credits would support its growth outlook. Further delays or reductions to contracted revenue would suggest that physical infrastructure, rather than demand for AI chips, is limiting Cerebras's growth.
