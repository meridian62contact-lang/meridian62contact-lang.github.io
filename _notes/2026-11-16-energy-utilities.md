### What happened

Constellation Energy (Nasdaq: CEG) reported third-quarter results on 9 November 2026, with <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-adjusted-operating-earnings-per-share">adjusted operating earnings</a> rising to $5.06 per share from $3.04 a year earlier [1]. Its update also highlighted October's 20-year agreement with Google, which supports more than $4.3bn of investment to add approximately 890 <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-megawatt-mw">megawatts (MW)</a> of nuclear generating capacity across 11 existing reactor units [1][4]. Alongside this, Constellation has signed a <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-conditional-loan-commitment">conditional US government loan commitment</a> of up to $5bn for nuclear upgrades, although a final loan agreement must still be completed before that financing becomes available [1].

### What people are missing

The evidence suggests the useful distinction is between securing a customer for electricity and creating the physical capacity to produce more of it [1][2]. Nuclear plants use heat from the reactor to make steam, which turns turbines connected to electricity generators, so their output depends on the performance of the whole system [2]. Constellation identifies two routes to increasing capacity, known as <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-uprate">uprating</a>: produce more heat in the reactor, or improve the equipment so that more electricity is extracted from the same heat [2]. Increasing heat output generally means higher steam and water flows, which can require upgrades to turbines and pumps as well as regulatory approval for the reactor's higher operating level [3]. The extra capacity therefore becomes usable only when the necessary equipment changes and approvals are in place, linking the commercial value of the agreement to the plant's engineering timetable [3].

### Why it matters

For Constellation, long customer commitments help make investment in existing plants worthwhile, giving the company greater confidence that there will be demand for the additional electricity over many years, but the additional electricity still depends on completing the work [1][2]. Delays would postpone the extra production and the income it could earn, while poorly managed upgrades could also mean longer shutdowns and less electricity from plants already operating [3]. We therefore see the main test as whether Constellation can increase supply while keeping its plants dependable, with the benefits of expansion judged by the extra electricity delivered after the work is finished [3].

### What we are watching next

We are watching whether Constellation maintains its target for the first Google-supported upgrade to begin delivering additional capacity in 2028, and whether the conditional government financing progresses to a completed loan agreement [1]. A clearer schedule for equipment installation and approvals would strengthen our reading that the contracts are becoming additional supply, while slippage would push the expected benefits further into the future [3].
