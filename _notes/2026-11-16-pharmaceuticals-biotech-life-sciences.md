### What happened

On 6 November 2026, the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) announced the launch of a safety investigation into Novartis' (NOVN) <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-gene-therapy">gene therapy</a> Zolgensma over serious <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-malignancy">malignancy</a> concerns [1]. The treatment was first approved in 2019 for <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-spinal-muscular-atrophy-sma">spinal muscular atrophy (SMA)</a> in children under two, and it uses an emptied virus as a carrier to deliver a replacement copy of the missing gene [2]. Novartis has recently disclosed nine tumour cases among 6,000+ previously treated patients, in two of which the delivered gene had inserted itself into the child's DNA next to a cancer-promoting gene and switched it on [3]. The cause of a third, similar case, is not yet known [1]. The FDA has kept the drug on the market while it reviews its safety concerns [3].

### What people are missing

Spinal muscular atrophy is an inherited genetic disorder caused by mutations in both copies of the <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-smn1-gene">SMN1 gene</a>, which encodes a protein essential for the survival and function of <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-motor-neurons">motor neurons</a>. Without it, the nerves controlling voluntary muscles deteriorate. In Type 1, this deterioration begins within months of birth, and weakening of respiratory muscles usually proves fatal within the first two years [4]. The emerging concern with Zolgensma [1] is not simply the number of reported tumours, but whether the mechanism itself can trigger cancer development. The therapy uses a harmless virus (<a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-aav9-vector">AAV9 vector</a>) to deliver a working SMN1 gene into motor neurons without altering the patient's existing DNA. In recent FDA investigations, two of the nine cases demonstrated that the vector's DNA had inserted itself within or upstream of <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-gli1">GLI1</a>, a growth-promoting gene, causing tumours from the abnormally accelerated growth [3]. However, this phenomenon (<a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-insertional-mutagenesis">insertional mutagenesis</a>) is a recognised risk of most gene therapies. This raises concerns for Itvisma, approved in November 2025, which uses the same vector and genetic construct but is administered <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-intrathecal-injection">into the spinal cord</a> rather than <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-intravenous-infusion">intravenously</a>. Although no tumours have been reported, its long-term safety remains uncertain given its recent approval.

### Why it matters

Although Zolgensma and Itvisma represent a relatively small part of Novartis' overall business, they are an important area of growth. Both treatments generated $404 million in sales in Q3 2026, an increase of about 35% from the same period last year, accounting for 2.8% of Novartis' total quarterly revenue [6]. Much of this growth came from the launch of Itvisma, meaning that any further safety concerns could affect the commercial prospect of both genetic treatments [6]. Given that each is a one-time gene therapy costing $2.1–2.5 million, the confidence in their long-term safety is an important factor for both healthcare providers and patients [7]. While the FDA has stated that the benefits of both treatments continue to outweigh their potential risks, any changes to their safety warnings or monitoring requirements could affect future uptake [1].

### What we are watching next

We are watching for the FDA's decisions on whether the current safety information and long-term follow-up studies are sufficient, or whether further warnings and monitoring requirements will be introduced, as well as seeing if the incident estimate across the 6,000+ treatments will be released [1][3]. The investigation into the cause of the third case will also be important in establishing whether the same mechanism is involved. More broadly, the FDA has confirmed that it is monitoring other AAV-based gene therapies for similar risks, so the outcome could have implications beyond Novartis' SMA treatments [1].
