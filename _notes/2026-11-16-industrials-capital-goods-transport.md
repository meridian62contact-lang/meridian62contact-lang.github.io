### What happened

Siemens reported record fourth-quarter orders of €34.8bn on 12 November, up 58% year-on-year, alongside an 11% rise in revenue to €23.7bn [1]. Most of the jump in orders came from trains rather than data centres: Mobility's orders rose from €2.5bn to €10.7bn, nearly two-thirds of the group's increase [1]. The data-centre story showed up instead in sales and cash, as Smart Infrastructure, the division that supplies electrical equipment to data centres, grew revenue 16% to €7.2bn and <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-free-cash-flow">free cash flow</a> 32% to €1.9bn [1]. Over the full year, Siemens' data-centre orders more than doubled to over €8.5bn and data-centre revenue rose about 60% to roughly €4.6bn, with data centres now making up about 20% of Smart Infrastructure's sales [2][3].

### What people are missing

In October we asked whether Siemens could turn data-centre orders into revenue as fast as it was winning them. The quarterly results now show the conversion happening, one to two quarters behind the orders. Smart Infrastructure's <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-comparable-basis">comparable</a> order growth jumped from 22% in the first fiscal quarter to 35% in the second, when Siemens booked a record €1.9bn of data-centre orders, and to 42% in the third, while its comparable revenue growth only accelerated afterwards, from 10% in the first two quarters to 13% in the third and 15% in the fourth [1][4][5][6][13].

Why these orders become revenue for Siemens comes down to the chip. A server <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-cpu">CPU</a> has a few dozen powerful cores built to switch between varied tasks and spends much of its time waiting for data, which is why conventional <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-rack">racks</a> were built for 8 to 12 <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-kw-mw">kW</a> and cooled by blowing air across them [7]. A <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-gpu">GPU</a> runs thousands of simpler cores on the same calculation in parallel, and AI workloads keep them near full load: one Nvidia Blackwell GPU is rated at 1,000 to 1,200 W, and a 72-GPU rack draws about 120 kW and is liquid-cooled [8][9]. Since almost all of that power leaves the chip as heat, removing 120 kW with a 10 °C temperature rise would take about 10 m³ of air every second, a 30 km/h wind through the front of the rack, against roughly 3 litres of water per second (our calculation). Siemens, however, is mostly paid to bring the power in rather than to take the heat out. Its data-centre wins sit in its electrification and electrical products businesses (control systems, <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-transformer">transformers</a>, switches, etc.), whose fourth-quarter orders rose 62% and 45%, and every kilowatt must pass through <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-switchgear">switchgear</a> and transformers whether it feeds a GPU or a CPU [2]. Siemens therefore earns per megawatt built rather than per chip, so its conventional data-centre business continues even if AI spending slows, although it does not disclose how its sales split between the two.

Much of next year is already contracted. Siemens expects to convert about 70% of its data-centre <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-backlog">backlog</a> of more than €7bn into revenue in FY2027; if data-centre revenue grows by the 40% the CFO said it could, about three-quarters of it is already in the order book [3]. Beyond that the protection thins: Siemens' multi-year agreement with Compass Datacenters guarantees delivery of "up to" 1,500 <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-medium-voltage">medium-voltage</a> power units over five years, a ceiling rather than a minimum purchase, and the CFO warned of possible "hesitation in order intake" as customers wait for <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-permitting">permits</a> [3][10].

The share price has not treated this as a new growth story. On 12 February, the day of its first-quarter results, Siemens closed at €257.00, or 25.3 times its previous twelve months' <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-earnings-per-share">earnings per share</a> of €10.14 [4][11]. On 12 November it closed at €281.70, or 25.7 times FY2026 earnings per share of €10.94 [1][12]. The price rose 9.6% while earnings per share rose 7.9%, so nearly all of the gain came from higher earnings rather than a higher <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-multiple">multiple</a>, even as data centres grew from roughly 3.6% to 5.5% of group revenue [1][3].

### Why it matters

A <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-p-e-ratio">P/E</a> of about 26 means a buyer pays 26 years of today's profit for each share, which only makes sense if profits are expected to keep growing; Siemens guides for 13% growth in earnings per share at the midpoint of its FY2027 range, excluding Siemens Healthineers [1]. A steady multiple suggests investors are paying for data-centre earnings as they arrive rather than in advance, which leaves less optimism to unwind if AI spending slows. It also means the market has not rewarded data centres, which could account for nearly a third of next year's expected revenue growth from under a tenth of revenue once Healthineers leaves [1][2][3]. The comparison has limits: the P/E covers the whole group, including trains and healthcare; one-off items distort it, as the 2025 Innomotics sale lowered Siemens' FY2025 P/E from about 24 to 19; the share price still includes Healthineers while the FY2027 guidance does not; and a single closing price also moves with interest rates and the wider market [1][11].

### What we are watching next

At Siemens' first-quarter FY2027 results in February 2027, we will be watching whether data-centre revenue keeps growing faster than data-centre orders, which would show the backlog converting, and whether the P/E moves clearly above 26, which would signal that investors have begun paying in advance for data-centre growth.
