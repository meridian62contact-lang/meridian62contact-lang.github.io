### What happened

Bangladeshi garment workers have been protesting for a higher minimum wage, demanding an increase from 12,500 taka to 30,000 taka per month, a 140% increase, to keep pace with inflation [3]. The protests have disrupted production across major manufacturing hubs, forcing nearly 150 factories to close according to the Business and Human Rights Centre [3]. Because the unrest hits during the peak pre-holiday export window, it raises instant concerns about shipment delays for international retailers, including Zara, H&M, Primark and Walmart [3][4]. The unrest has also affected transport networks and factory operations, with workers blocking major highways and clashing with police and paramilitary forces [2][3]. Bangladesh's garment industry employs approximately 4 million people and generates around US$40 billion in annual export earnings, making it the world's second-largest apparel exporter behind China [2][4].

### What people are missing

The deeper issue extends beyond whether retailers face short-term shipping delays. It exposes a systemic flaw in fast fashion's overall pricing model, with Bangladesh at its forefront. The low-cost model relies on a large workforce accepting wages that fail to keep pace with increasing living costs. Reuters reported that inflation has averaged around 9% since 2022, while workers have reduced spending on food, healthcare and education [1].

This creates a supply-chain risk for retailers. If wage negotiations remain unresolved, factories may close or operate below capacity, derailing brands' delivery schedules. If wages rise sharply with no brand support, local manufacturers lose part of the cost advantage that makes Bangladesh attractive compared with regional rivals like India and Vietnam [4]. Retailers will then face a choice: absorb higher wholesale prices, renegotiate contracts, or shift production elsewhere.

### Why it matters

The disruption challenges the assumption that low-cost sourcing automatically equals efficient sourcing. A supply chain may offer low production costs but still become expensive if strikes, delays or factory closures force retailers to use faster transport, find replacement suppliers or discount products that arrive late. Factory closures can delay clothing reaching shops, reduce the time available to sell seasonal collections and make it harder for brands to respond to changes in customer demand.

For retailers, supplier resilience and labour relations are core commercial drivers, not just corporate social responsibility (CSR) considerations. Brands also face acute reputational costs and regulatory issues if workers' wages remain insufficient or if production continues under unsafe or unstable conditions. In this sense, Bangladesh creates a double risk: prolonged strikes can disrupt sales and inventory, while inadequate responses to workers' demands can damage consumer trust and expose retailers to criticism over how their products are made.

### What we are watching next

The next indicators are whether the wage board reaches an agreement, how quickly closed factories resume production and whether retailers disclose delays to spring collections [3][4]. A negotiated wage increase alongside stable production would suggest that Bangladesh can preserve its role as a reliable sourcing base. Continued closures or a shift of orders towards India and Vietnam, however, would indicate that labour unrest is beginning to reshape global fashion supply chains.
