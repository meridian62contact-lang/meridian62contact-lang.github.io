### What happened

Taiwan <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-semiconductor">Semiconductor</a> Manufacturing Company (NYSE: TSM) is investing heavily in the US at a time when Washington and Beijing are fighting for control of the <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-semiconductor-supply-chain">semiconductor supply chain</a>. In July, TSMC announced another $100bn investment in Arizona, bringing its total planned investment in the US to $265bn [1]. One reason this matters is Taiwan's position in the conflict: China has been increasing its military activity around the island, where TSMC makes many of the world's most advanced chips [2]. At the same time, the US has introduced <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-export-controls">export controls</a> to prevent China from accessing advanced chips, manufacturing equipment and <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-high-bandwidth-memory-hbm">high-bandwidth memory</a> [3]. The US is therefore trying to do two things at once: slow down China's progress in advanced semiconductors and become less dependent on Taiwan for its own chip supply.

### What people are missing

The semiconductor industry is not controlled by one country. Instead, it depends on a small number of companies, each specialising in a different part of the production process. American companies like Nvidia design AI chips but rely on TSMC to manufacture them, and TSMC itself depends on other companies, including the Dutch firm ASML, the only commercial supplier of <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-euv-lithography">extreme ultraviolet (EUV) lithography</a> machines. These machines are used to print the smallest features on advanced chips, and US-led export restrictions have prevented China from buying them [4].

But having the right machines is only part of the challenge. Making a semiconductor involves hundreds of steps, and manufacturers need years of experience to improve their processes and increase the number of working chips they get from each silicon <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-wafer">wafer</a>. This is where TSMC has a major advantage. Over several decades, it has built a network of factories in Taiwan that work closely together; it can also reuse equipment from older manufacturing generations and move production between facilities to make better use of its existing <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-manufacturing-capacity">manufacturing capacity</a> [5]. This helps TSMC increase output without having to build an entirely new factory every time demand rises.

These capabilities matter financially because <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-manufacturing-yield">manufacturing yield</a> directly affects the cost per usable chip. If a wafer produces fewer working chips, its production costs must be spread across fewer saleable units, increasing the cost of each chip. TSMC's accumulated manufacturing expertise therefore gives it an advantage not just in production efficiency, but potentially in profitability as well. Replicating its factories overseas is not simply a matter of buying the same equipment; it also means reproducing the processes and experience needed to operate that equipment efficiently at scale. This is what makes the US–China rivalry more complicated than it first appears: China cannot easily replace the foreign technologies it is being denied, but the US cannot simply build a few factories in Arizona and expect to reproduce everything TSMC has developed in Taiwan.

### Why it matters

Taiwan's importance in semiconductor manufacturing makes it a major risk for companies that depend on TSMC. If a military conflict or blockade were to disrupt production or shipments from the island, the effects could be felt across the global technology industry. Building factories in Arizona helps reduce this dependence and brings chip production closer to American customers such as Nvidia, AMD and Apple [6]. However, diversification comes at a cost: building new factories requires substantial upfront <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-capital-expenditure">capital expenditure</a>, long before those facilities generate revenue, and once production begins, their profitability depends on manufacturing yields, <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-operating-costs">operating costs</a> and how fully their capacity is used. Overseas expansion could therefore improve supply security while putting pressure on <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-cash-flow">cash flow</a> and <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-gross-margin">gross margins</a>, particularly while new facilities are ramping up. This creates a difficult balance for TSMC: it must expand outside Taiwan to make its supply chain more resilient without undermining the manufacturing efficiency that underpins its competitive advantage.

### What we are watching next

TSMC will publish its third-quarter results on 15 October [7]. We will be watching management's guidance on capital expenditure, the financial impact of overseas expansion and the progress of the Arizona ramp — gross-margin guidance in particular, since it can help show whether the costs of expanding internationally are pressuring overall profitability, though TSMC may not disclose <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-arizona-specific-margins">Arizona-specific margins</a> or yields. We will also look for measurable evidence that Arizona's operations are becoming more efficient. The central question is whether TSMC can diversify production while preserving the manufacturing economics behind its competitive advantage.
