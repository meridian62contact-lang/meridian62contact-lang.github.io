### What happened

On 6 October 2026 the <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-chancellor">Chancellor</a>, John Healey, met bank bosses [1] in talks that City AM had said would include Lloyds Banking Group (LLOY) [2], after reports that the <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-treasury">Treasury</a> was considering a time-limited tax on bank profits for his first <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-budget">Budget</a> on 28 October 2026 [3]. Days after the government's <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-30-year-borrowing-costs">30-year borrowing costs</a> hit their highest level since 1998 amid worries about oil prices and inflation [4], he gave no hint of a tax rise, warning that the UK's finances were "challenging" [1]. The common assumption is that higher interest rates and a government short of money are both bad news for Lloyds.

### What people are missing

Our finance and accounting training suggests Lloyds has less to fear from swings in interest rates than from the Budget, because it uses a <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-structural-hedge">structural hedge</a> to fix the interest it earns on £246bn of steady money, such as <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-current-account">current-account</a> balances, for several years at a time [5]. Without the hedge, that money would earn whatever rates happened to be at the time, but the fixed deals last about 3.75 years on average, so a rate change only reaches the part being renewed while the rest keeps its fixed rate [5]. As old low-rate deals are replaced at today's higher rates, the hedge's income has risen gradually, to £3.4bn in the first half of 2026 from £2.6bn a year earlier [5]. Lloyds' modelling suggests that a 0.25 <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-percentage-point">percentage point</a> rise across all interest rates would add only about £75m in the first year and a cut of the same size would cost about £100m, against more than £14.9bn it expects to earn from interest this year after paying savers and other lenders [5]. A tax would come straight off profit, and banks already pay 28% on most of their profits against 25% for other large companies [6], an <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-extra-3-charge">extra 3%</a> that cost Lloyds £94m in the first half of 2026 [5], so we read a new tax as the risk that would reach its profits first.

### Why it matters

Lloyds describes itself as the UK's largest financial services provider, with 28 million customers and the number one position in current accounts [5], so any new bank tax would land on the bank many households use for their everyday money. In 1981 Chancellor Geoffrey Howe announced a one-off tax on bank deposits that paid customers no interest, saying high interest rates had driven much of the banks' profit, especially on current accounts [7]. We read this as a precedent for taxing Lloyds, whose locked-in deals earn on the same kind of money today [5]. Banking analyst John Cronin said, as reported by City AM, that UK lenders would "rightly be furious" if a new tax spared the UK arms of international banks [2], and in that case we read Lloyds as one of the lenders that would carry more of the cost.

### What we are watching next

We are watching the Budget on 28 October 2026 [8], although the Telegraph reports that senior ministers are against a new tax on banks [9]. The structure of the tax will matter, because raising the extra 3% charge to 5% or back to 8%, options City AM lists [1], would cut Lloyds' profits every year it stays, while the time-limited tax the Treasury was reported to be considering (similar to the tax on oil and gas firms after the Ukraine war began) would end after a set period [3]. Either would bear out our reading, while a Budget with no new bank tax would weaken it, since the risk we flag would have passed, leaving Lloyds' locked-in deals (which it expects to earn more than £7bn this year) to keep supporting its profits [5].
