### What happened

USD/JPY briefly breached the "line-in-the-sand" threshold of ¥160 on 12 November, following hotter-than-expected US <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-inflation">inflation</a> strengthening bets that the <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-federal-reserve">Federal Reserve</a> will consequently raise interest rates [1]. Japan's chief currency official, Atsushi Mimura, said recent currency moves had been "too fast" and that it was too soon to forget the summer's <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-intervention">intervention</a> [1]. Between 30 July and 3 August, Japanese authorities intervened in the currency market three times, including Tokyo and Washington conducting their first <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-joint-intervention">joint intervention</a> since 1998, spending a total of ¥15.4 trillion (US$96.4 billion) to target disorderly and excessively fast market movements following a steep decline in the yen towards ¥164 per USD — the weakest in 39 years [2][3][4].

### What people are missing

Spending is unlikely to fix the underlying weakness of the yen alone. While singular interventions move the exchange rate once, the interest-rate gap rewards the opposite trade every day. Despite the <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-bank-of-japan">Bank of Japan</a>'s gradual rate hikes, US interest rates remain significantly higher, giving investors a stronger return for holding dollar-denominated assets [5][6]. The Fed's <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-policy-rate-target-range">target range</a> is 3.75%–4.00% and the Bank of Japan's policy rate is 1.25%, a gap of about 2.5 to 2.75 <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-percentage-point">percentage points</a> that any intervention leaves untouched [5][6]. As a result, many global investors continue to borrow in yen and invest in higher-yielding US bonds and <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-money-market-instruments">money-market instruments</a>, a strategy known as <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-carry-trading">carry trading</a> [7]. Japan's record ¥15.4tn of purchases left USD/JPY only around ¥4 below its late-July level [1]. Meanwhile, interest-rate futures put about a 91% probability on the Fed raising interest rates by a <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-quarter-point">quarter-point</a> in December, which would widen the gap further [8]. Currency intervention may temporarily support the yen, but as long as this yield advantage persists, market participants have a financial incentive to favour dollars over yen.

### Why it matters

Japanese authorities can step in again, but each intervention uses up public money without changing fundamentally why the yen is weak. The yen's direction over the coming weeks may depend more on interest-rate decisions in Washington and Tokyo, rather than on any single purchase by the Japanese government. For Japanese households and businesses, a weaker yen means paying more yen for anything priced in dollars, so that pressure is felt in everyday costs whether or not governments step in.

### What we are watching next

The change in the interest-rate gap after the Federal Reserve's decision on 9 December and the Bank of Japan's decision on 18 December, where board members' comments point to a good chance of a rise [9].
