### What happened

On 21 October, the ONS reported that UK <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-inflation">inflation</a> rose to 3.5% in September, up from 3.1% in August, with motor fuels the biggest driver [1]. Economists had expected 3.4% [2]. The 10-year <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-gilt-yield">gilt yield</a> stayed around 5.4% this week, close to multi-year highs, ahead of Chancellor John Healey's first <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-budget">Budget</a> on Wednesday 28 October [3][4].

### What people are missing

Most coverage focuses on gilt yields, the interest rate the government pays when it borrows by selling <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-gilt">gilts</a>. But a rise in yields only affects new borrowing. Most existing debt pays a fixed rate until it is repaid, so higher yields feed into the government's <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-debt-interest">debt interest</a> bill slowly, over years. <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-index-linked-gilt">Index-linked gilts</a> are different. Their interest payments, and the amount repaid at the end, rise with the <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-retail-prices-index-rpi">Retail Prices Index (RPI)</a>, with a lag of about three months [5]. The government had around £692bn of these bonds outstanding at the end of June 2026 [6]. This means an inflation surprise like September's raises the government's costs within months, much faster than a rise in gilt yields does. It also adds to pressure that already exists: August borrowing was £3.5bn above the <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-office-for-budget-responsibility-obr">Office for Budget Responsibility</a>'s forecast [7].

### Why it matters

Money spent on debt interest cannot be spent on public services or used to cut taxes. If inflation stays high, the Chancellor could have less room in the Budget even if market borrowing costs stop rising. The price rises that squeeze households' budgets also squeeze the government's.

### What we are watching next

On 28 October, the OBR's forecast published alongside the Budget should show how much of the change in debt interest comes from inflation rather than higher gilt yields [4]. On 5 November, the Bank of England sets interest rates, having held <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-bank-rate">Bank Rate</a> at 3.75% in September in a 6-3 vote [8].
