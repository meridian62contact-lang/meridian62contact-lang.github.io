### What happened

<a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-spot-gold">Spot gold</a> fell by around 3% on 28 September 2026 to roughly $4,150 per ounce, its lowest level since 5 August, after a surge in crude oil renewed concerns about inflation [1]. Gold is widely held as a hedge against inflation, so an inflation scare would ordinarily support its price — instead it did the opposite. Over the same period, markets moved to price a more than 70% chance of a further increase in the <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-federal-funds-rate">federal funds rate</a> at the October meeting, following the Federal Reserve's decision on 16 September 2026 to raise the target range to 3.75%–4.00% [2][3].

### What people are missing

Gold's fall is being read as a signal about inflation expectations, but gold is a poor instrument for that purpose, because several variables moved at once: oil, the dollar and real yields all shifted on the same day, and the gold price responds to all of them together. Isolating expected inflation requires a comparison in which only that variable differs, and the Treasury market provides one, since conventional and inflation-protected bonds of the same maturity differ principally in whether they compensate the holder for inflation. The gap between them, the ten-year breakeven rate, began September 2026 at 2.35% and stood at 2.34% on 28 September, moving within a range of roughly seven basis points across the month [4]. Over the same period the nominal ten-year <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-yield">yield</a> rose from 4.78% to 5.24%, so almost the whole of that 46 <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-basis-point">basis point</a> increase came from <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-real-yield">real yields</a> rather than from expected inflation, and the two-year rose further still, by 54 basis points [5]. On that evidence we read September as a repricing of expected policy rather than of long-run inflation, which is a more reliable reading than the gold price alone can give.

### Why it matters

If markets are responding to what the Federal Reserve is expected to do, rather than to inflation itself, the effect shows up first in the price of the dollar. Higher expected American interest rates draw money towards dollar assets, and the <a class="glossary-term" href="/glossary/#term-dollar-index">dollar index</a> has strengthened above 101, close to its highest level this year [5]. For anyone outside the United States, that means American goods, oil and other commodities priced in dollars, and dollar debts, all cost more — which is how a decision taken in Washington reaches households elsewhere.

### What we are watching next

The Federal Open Market Committee meets on 27–28 October 2026, and the figure we are watching is the ten-year breakeven rate, which closed September at 2.35%. If it begins to rise while nominal yields hold, the reading above no longer holds, and the market would be telling us something about long-run inflation instead.
