Follow the money

Every company on the map is ultimately paid from two pools: the cash flows of the four US hyperscalers (Microsoft, Alphabet, Amazon, Meta) plus Oracle, and the money that investors put into AI labs and neoclouds. AI labs' revenue is growing fast, but their compute commitments are many times larger. Anthropic reported H1 2026 revenue of $16.3bn, while its S-1 reportedly lists roughly $800bn of multi-year compute contracts.21 OpenAI's annualised revenue was reported above $25bn in April 2026, against a single Oracle contract of about $300bn.22,23

Those commitments become the contracted backlogs of the clouds: Oracle reported $664bn of remaining performance obligations at Q1 FY2027, roughly half reportedly tied to OpenAI, and CoreWeave $132.5bn.18,19 The clouds turn backlog into capex (Oracle guides $90–95bn for FY2027, CoreWeave $37–39bn for 2026, TSMC $60–64bn), and that capex becomes the orders of everyone upstream: Vertiv's organic orders more than doubled in Q3 2026, Siemens' data-centre backlog passed €7bn, and ASML holds €9.3bn of customer deposits.2,3,4,17,18,19

The key point for analysis: contracts get firmer the closer a company sits to the data centre (leases, take-or-pay compute, power purchase agreements) and softer upstream (purchase orders, spot metal prices). But a contract is only as good as the party paying it. Much of the downstream backlog is ultimately owed by loss-making AI labs, so "contracted" revenue at Oracle or CoreWeave carries credit risk that a gas contract with TSMC does not.

Circular financing

Several suppliers fund their own customers. Nvidia has invested in OpenAI, CoreWeave and Anthropic, each of which buys Nvidia GPUs; AMD gave OpenAI warrants over AMD shares in exchange for a 6 GW GPU commitment; Microsoft, Amazon and Alphabet invest in AI labs that then spend the money on their clouds. These loops can be rational (they secure demand and lower the lab's cost of capital), but they also mean revenue on one side partly depends on equity raised on the other. Growth vs value in this chain

A growth stock is priced mainly on profits it is expected to earn years from now, so its P/E is high and small changes in the growth outlook move the share price a lot. A value stock is priced mainly on what it earns today, so less optimism is built in. Cyclicals are a third group: their profits swing with a commodity or capital-spending cycle, so investors value them on "mid-cycle" earnings and their P/E often looks lowest exactly when profits are about to fall.

In this chain the labels follow a pattern. The scarcest, hardest-to-copy positions (ASML's EUV monopoly, Nvidia's software ecosystem, Arm's royalties, Vertiv's data-centre focus) trade as growth stocks. Long-contracted, utility-like businesses (Linde, Air Liquide, data-centre REITs) trade as blend or value. Commodity producers and memory makers trade as cyclicals because their prices, not their volumes, decide profits. The neoclouds and Oracle are unusual: they have long contracts (normally a value trait) but are valued as growth stocks because their contracts are growing quickly and are funded with debt.

The labels on this map describe how the market typically treats each company's business, not a current valuation call.
